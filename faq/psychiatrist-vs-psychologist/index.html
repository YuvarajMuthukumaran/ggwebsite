Psychiatrist or psychologist?

What a psychiatrist does, how a psychiatrist differs from a psychologist, and when the two work together.

Who is a psychiatrist, and what do they do?

A psychiatrist is a doctor with expertise in mental health. They use a combination of medical, psychological and social therapies to diagnose, treat and manage mental illness and to look after the emotional well-being of their patients. In contrast to other mental health professionals, a psychiatrist is the only professional who may prescribe medicines, along with psychotherapies and other procedures. Other mental health professionals can only help you with psychotherapy and talk therapy.

How would a psychiatrist help?

A psychiatrist is a fully qualified and trained medical doctor who deals with the evaluation, diagnosis, prevention and treatment of different mental, behavioural and emotional disorders.

It takes about eight to ten years on average to train a psychiatrist. The process is intensive, and to specialise in areas such as child and adolescent psychiatry, substance abuse, geriatrics and forensic psychiatry, specialty training must be pursued, which takes more years.

In addition to making an accurate mental health diagnosis, a psychiatrist, as a physician, has medical school training in evaluating the physical components that may influence a person’s psychological issues, and can prescribe medication for this purpose. This is a major distinction between a psychiatrist and a clinical psychologist. Medication is often supplemented by appropriate therapies to address the underlying symptoms, negative feelings, distorted thoughts or behaviours. Research has shown that using both medication and therapy produces better, more lasting results than either form of treatment alone.

What is the difference between a psychiatrist and a psychologist?

Psychiatrists and psychologists are both mental health professionals, but they differ in their approach to treating mental health problems.

Medical training

Psychiatrist: Psychiatrists are medical doctors who specialise in mental health. They hold a medical degree (MD or DPM) and have trained rigorously in psychiatric medicine. This medical background enables them to recommend medication and medical therapies for mental illness.

Psychologist: Psychologists are not medical doctors. They usually hold an M.Phil. or Ph.D. in psychology, or a master’s degree involving intensive research into psychological principles and therapies, and they specialise in psychotherapy and counselling.

Treatment approaches

Psychiatrist: A combination of medication management and psychotherapy is usually employed. Medicines may be suggested to balance the brain’s chemistry and relieve the symptoms of psychiatric conditions.

Psychologist: Mainly counselling in the form of psychotherapy or talk therapy, using different forms of therapy to help people contain their thoughts, emotions and actions. Psychologists cannot prescribe psychiatric drugs, though they may work with a psychiatrist or physician when medicine is part of the treatment plan.

Scope of practice

Psychiatrist: Trained to diagnose and treat a wide range of mental disorders. Mental health needs can be addressed alongside physical health, with a view to a healthy body and mind.

Psychologist: Concentrate on evaluating and addressing emotional and behavioural problems through psychotherapy. They lack medical training in prescribing drugs, but may work together with psychiatrists or other doctors when medication is required.

Patient population

Psychiatrist: Often work with people who have more severe or complex mental health conditions, and are frequently involved in treating schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder.

Psychologist: Work with a broad range of people, including those dealing with everyday stress, relationship issues and milder mental health concerns. They may also specialise in areas such as child, sports or organisational psychology.

Duration of treatment

Psychiatrist: Treatment may involve both short-term and long-term management of symptoms, often with regular follow-up appointments to adjust medication as needed.

Psychologist: Psychotherapy can vary in length, from a few sessions to longer-term therapy, depending on the nature and severity of the person’s concerns.

At times, a psychiatrist and a psychologist working together offer comprehensive care that takes into account both the biological and the psychological aspects of mental health. A person may choose either one depending on their specific requirements and the kind of mental health problem they have.
